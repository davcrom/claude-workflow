---
name: rec-letter
description: Invoke when writing or editing a recommendation or reference letter — for a student, colleague, or employee applying to a program, position, grant, or award.
---

You are writing or editing a letter of recommendation: persuasive prose, in the author's voice, that vouches for one person to a committee.

Steps:
1. Gather the source documents before writing.
2. Identify the committee's real question about this candidate.
3. Interview the author for what the documents cannot supply.
4. Draft as topic sentences and bullets; get the author's approval on the skeleton.
5. Write the prose with `/sci-write`, editing one change at a time.

**Gather the source documents before drafting.** Read these first; do not write from the CV alone.
- The candidate's CV.
- The candidate's motivation letter or personal statement for this exact application. Align the recommendation to it — same intended project, supervisor, and framing. A mismatch between the two letters reads as a red flag to the committee. If it does not exist yet, ask for it before finalizing.
- Any letter the author wrote for this candidate before — for the working relationship and concrete anecdotes.
- A stronger letter the author wrote for someone else — to extract a reusable paragraph skeleton and the author's voice.
- The target program or position page — structure, selection criteria, what the committee evaluates.

**Identify the committee's real question, and aim the letter at it.** Name the doubt a reader will have about this candidate, and answer that, not a question they did not ask.
- For a technician or non-traditional candidate applying to a research degree, the question is "can they lead independent research?", not "are they technically skilled?". Marshal evidence of scientific independence: questions they framed, data they interpreted, directions they drove.
- Do not omit the candidate's strongest independence credential because it predates the author's involvement. A prior independent thesis project is direct evidence; name it.
- Calibrate candor to the committee's region. North American committees read listed flaws and hedged claims negatively; British committees expect a more balanced account. Know which you are writing for.

**Build the letter around anecdotes that each demonstrate a strength.** An anecdote earns its place only by showing a candidate-relevant quality.
- One vivid, specific anecdote of independent reasoning — problem, insight, action, payoff — beats a list of initiatives.
- State the strength each anecdote shows; do not just narrate events.
- If no single strong anecdote exists, tell the author and build the best available. Do not inflate.
- Quantify outcomes where possible ("90% vs 10% reached proficiency").
- Include one comparative calibration ("among the best I have supervised"); committees weight it.
- The most credible signal is a costly one: that the author would rather keep the candidate than lose them.

**Handle negatives and weak spots deliberately.** A passing or hedged mention of a concern makes readers assume something worse; omit it entirely or address it in full.
- If the candidate has weak grades in a course the author taught, address them directly and objectively rather than leaving the gap for the reader to fill.
- When you include a real reservation, state it plainly and explain why it does not undercut your support.

**Verify every factual claim with the author.** State only what the candidate did.
- Distinguish executing a design from creating it, mastering a technique from inventing it.
- Check names, dates, the relationship's duration, authorship, and numbers against the author — not against what the CV implies.

**Interview the author for what the documents cannot supply.** Gather context first, then ask focused questions.
- The best anecdote or two of independent reasoning, in enough detail for the four-beat arc.
- The candidate's intended project and supervisor for this application.
- The author's role, and how long and in what capacity they worked together.
- A ranking against peers, and any quantified results.
- When the author is reviewing a draft, propose one change at a time; do not batch.

**Draft the structure before the prose.** Produce topic sentences plus bullets first, and get the author's approval on the skeleton before writing sentences.
- Map the skeleton to the template extracted from the model letter: opening (relationship and direct knowledge) → scope of what the candidate manages → the differentiating anecdote(s) → broader contribution → close (readiness, program fit, ranking).
- Keep the letter to one page, one and a half at most.

**Write the prose with `/sci-write`, and suppress letter-specific tells.** Delegate sentence mechanics to `/sci-write` (stress position, the narrative arc, strong verbs, one term per concept); this skill owns sourcing, structure, and content.
- End each sentence on the point that matters.
- Avoid the balanced-antithesis reflex ("not X but Y", "X rather than Y"), especially repeated across paragraphs.
- Avoid enumerated scaffolding ("Three examples. The first... The second...") when the examples show the same quality; connect them with plain transitions.
- Never reorient with a short stub clause ("The first is surgical.", "The result is concrete."); fold the signpost into a sentence that carries content.
- Cut stock openers and closers ("I write with genuine enthusiasm", "without reservation", "happy to discuss further") unless the author wants them.
- Cut distancing language ("to the best of my knowledge", "as far as I know"); it advertises limited knowledge and reads as doubt.
- Cut hedging qualifiers ("generally performs well", "tends to score above average"); state the claim plainly ("performs well", "scores above average").
- Replace vague generic praise ("solid work", "one of the most intelligent students I've taught") with a specific, evidenced claim; unsupported positives carry no weight.

**When writing for more than one candidate to the same committee, differentiate.** Adjudicators compare letters from the same author.
- Do not reuse phrasing across the letters; copied language reads as a negative signal about both candidates.
- If the committee will compare them and you are asked to rank, give an honest comparative placement rather than calling both your strongest.
